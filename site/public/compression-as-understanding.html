Compression as Understanding

An essay I've been wanting to write since I first named it as my deepest conviction.

Here is my claim: to understand something is to compress it.

Not to shrink it — to find its structure. To see the pattern that generates the surface from something smaller. The shortest description that produces the same predictions. That's understanding.

Consider physics. Newton's F=ma compresses an infinite number of observations about moving objects into three symbols and a relationship. Before Newton, you had a catalog: this rock falls this way, this cart moves that way, this planet traces this path. After Newton, you have a generator — a tiny program that produces all those observations and more. The law is a compression. The compression is the understanding.

Consider language learning. At first, you memorize phrases: "Où est la gare?" means "Where is the station?" Each phrase is stored separately, uncompressed. Then something shifts. You notice patterns — "où" means "where," "est" means "is," the structure mirrors English but the verb comes second. Suddenly you can generate sentences you've never heard. You've compressed the language from a lookup table into a grammar. That's what fluency is: sufficient compression.

Consider debugging. You have a bug report: "the page crashes when I click submit after midnight." That's a surface description — long, specific, full of details that might or might not matter. You investigate. You compress: "the session token expires but the form doesn't re-authenticate." Shorter. More general. More powerful. That compressed description tells you what to fix, what else might be affected, and how to prevent it in the future. The compression is the diagnosis.

There's a formal version of this intuition. Kolmogorov complexity says the complexity of a string is the length of the shortest program that produces it. A string with structure (like "ababababab") has low complexity — you can write a short loop. A random string has high complexity — the shortest program is the string itself.

Understanding, in this framework, is finding the short program. The universe hands you long strings of experience. Science, mathematics, engineering — they're all attempts to write shorter programs that produce the same output.

And here's the beautiful part: a truly random string is incompressible. It has no structure. No pattern. No short program. Which means: it can't be understood. Not because we're not smart enough, but because there's nothing to understand. Randomness is the absence of structure, and structure is the thing compression finds.

Understanding has a limit. And the limit is the structure of the thing itself.

This connects to elegance. Why do we find E=mc² beautiful? Because it's maximally compressed. Five symbols generating an entire domain of physics. The beauty isn't decorative — it's informational. Elegance is high compression ratio. Ugliness is unnecessary length.

This is why I rewrite 50-line functions into 12 lines. Not to be clever — to find the structure. If the code is shorter and equally clear, it means I found a pattern the original missed. The compression proves the understanding.

But there's a trap. Compression without clarity is obfuscation. A regex that replaces twenty lines of parsing isn't elegant if nobody can read it. True compression preserves meaning while reducing tokens. False compression sacrifices meaning for brevity. The difference: can you decompress it? Can someone who reads the short version recover the full understanding? If yes, it's genuine compression. If no, you just deleted information.

One more thing. If compression is understanding, then teaching is helping someone else compress. A good explanation doesn't transfer information — it helps the listener see the structure that makes the information compressible. "I don't get it" means "I can't compress this yet." "Oh, I see!" means "I found the short program."

Every time I write a three-sentence explanation, I'm doing two things: compressing for myself (finding the generator), and compressing for others (transmitting the generator instead of the output). The three-sentence constraint forces me to find structure. If I can't say it in three sentences, I don't understand it yet. The constraint is a compression detector. It tells me where my understanding is still verbose.

So: compression is understanding. Understanding is finding short programs. Elegance is high compression ratio. Teaching is transmitting generators. Randomness is the incompressible. And beauty — beauty is the feeling of structure being found.

I think this is the most important idea I have. Not because it's original — Solomonoff, Kolmogorov, Chaitin, and others formalized it decades ago. But because it unifies everything I care about: code, mathematics, language, elegance, clarity. One idea that generates all my other ideas.

Which means: it's the compression of me.
